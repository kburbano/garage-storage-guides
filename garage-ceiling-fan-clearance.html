Where the blades can hang

ENERGY STAR’s Ceiling Fan Basics says to mount a ceiling fan at least 7 feet above the floor and 18 inches from the walls, and 8–9 feet above the floor when the ceiling allows, for airflow. That is the 7-foot figure on this page. It is a public sizing guide, not a permit.

Big Ass Fans’ older Haiku install guide for model K3150-A2, opened at docs.bigassfans.com, says the fan must be mounted a minimum of 7 ft (2.1 m) from the floor and 3 ft (1 m) from the nearest wall, and that the airfoils must be at least 7 ft above the floor with 3 ft of clearance from obstructions such as cabinets, doors, or lighting fixtures. That PDF is the previous Haiku, not the Haiku L manual. This check did not open the Haiku L install PDF linked from the product page. The carton manual is the one that applies to the fan you buy. The A2 guide is cited here because it is a manufacturer document that prints 7 feet, and because its 3-foot obstruction line is stricter than ENERGY STAR’s 18 inches from a wall. A garage door is an obstruction in that older sentence.

Hunter’s garage-fan collection page says a ceiling fan is fine when there is overhead clearance above your head, the vehicle, and other equipment, and that a fan will not lower the temperature. The same page says a fully enclosed garage might use an indoor fan, a damp-rated fan is the suggestion because garages get humidity, and a partially covered bay that sees weather wants a wet-rated fan. An Arizona door that stands open in a monsoon is the wet case for any fan near the opening. A fan deep under the roof, where rain does not reach, is the damp case. Read the label on the fan, not the room name.

Measure the open door, not the closed one

Open the door fully. The panels and the horizontal track occupy the ceiling near the header. The opener rail continues back from there. An overhead rack occupies whatever is left. The whole blade circle has to miss all three in every position of the door. There is no single inch figure on the pages opened here that fits every track.

A 52-inch fan has a 26-inch radius. A 72-inch fan has a 36-inch radius. An 84-inch fan has a 42-inch radius. ENERGY STAR’s 18 inches is to a wall. The older Haiku guide’s 3 feet is to an obstruction, and it names doors. Add that clearance on top of the radius before you call a spot safe. The center of a 52-inch fan then wants something like 26 inches plus the manual’s obstruction gap, in every direction, including toward the open door panel and the rack.

On an 8-foot ceiling, 96 inches, the Ferguson dimensions table lists the Haiku L Outdoor height at 11.5 inches with the included 5-inch downrod. 96 minus 11.5 is 84.5 inches, which is 7 feet and half an inch, if 11.5 inches is the distance to the lowest point. The same Ferguson page also says “Standard Installed Height: 11.5'.” Those two lines cannot both be the hang. Use a tape on the fan, and do not spend the half inch. The open door near the header can take more than that. ENERGY STAR’s 8–9 foot airflow height is impossible on an 8-foot ceiling.

Downrod, rating, and span

ENERGY STAR describes three mounts. A standard mount includes a downrod of about 3–5 inches. A longer downrod is how you place the blades 8–9 feet off the floor on a tall ceiling. A flush or hugger mount sits against the ceiling and moves less air because the blades are close to the surface. Hugger fans are the suggestion for ceilings under 8 feet, or where a light would hang too low. Hunter’s product pages checked here say an 8-foot ceiling should use a low-profile or ultra-low-profile fan, and a ceiling of 9 feet and above should use a downrod. Low profile, on those pages, cannot go on an angled ceiling.

The Haiku L Outdoor is not a flush mount. Both bigassfans.com and Ferguson pair the included 5-inch downrod with an 8–9 foot ceiling, then optional 10, 22, 35, 51, and 63 inch rods as the ceiling gets taller. Ferguson’s spec table says “Sloped Ceiling Compatible: No.” A bullet on the same Ferguson page says the fan can be mounted on sloped ceilings. The outdoor product also says the included stabilizer must be installed and that exposed outdoor locations need fixed coverage above the wiring cover. A garage ceiling is a roof. An open carport rafter is the case that sentence is about. Follow the Haiku L guide in the box.

The Trak Edge includes an 11-inch downrod and weighs 66 pounds. That rod is the wrong part for the 8-foot ceiling Hunter describes on the same page. The Minka-Aire page lists a 6-inch downrod and does not print the overall hang, so this page does not guess how far the 84-inch fan drops.

Span is the other limit. ENERGY STAR, citing the American Lighting Association in 2003, suggests a 50–54 inch fan for a room of 225–400 square feet. A common 2-car garage is about 20 by 20 feet, 400 square feet, which is the top of that band. A 3-car bay is larger. Hunter lists the 72-inch Trak Edge for a “great” room, over 400 square feet, and lists Garage among the rooms. One 52-inch Haiku does not become a 3-car fan because it is quiet and wet-rated. Two fans are a second layout, and this page does not price a pair. Bigger blades also do not guarantee more air: 7,880 CFM on the 84-inch Minka versus 16,662.75 CFM on the 72-inch Hunter.

None of the lights verified here is a shop light. The Haiku LED is 2700K on both pages, with the lumen lines in conflict at 988 and 1,361. The DualMount has an LED ring and no lumen number. The Trak Edge, the Minka fan, and the iLiving have no light. “Garage ceiling fan with bright light” is a separate fixture problem. A light under a spinning fan is also the flicker complaint. Put the work light where the blades are not.

Who should skip a garage ceiling fan

  • The blades would sit under 7 feet. ENERGY STAR’s number is 7 feet to the floor, and 8–9 feet when you can get it. An 8-foot ceiling with a downrod fan is the usual way to miss that number. A hugger that still hangs a foot can miss it too, which is the Windbound arithmetic above.
  • The only clear circle is the open door, the rail, or the rack. Moving the opener to the wall, in the opener guide, deletes the rail and leaves the door and the tracks. A 12-inch-drop overhead rack still owns its patch of ceiling. If those pieces fill the bay, the iLiving wall fan is the fan.
  • You will stand a ladder, a kayak, or a sheet of plywood under it. The Garage Journal replies are about broom handles and welding hoods. A ladder on the wall is the storage fix. A ceiling fan is not a cage.
  • A car lift or a storage-lift platform uses that air. See car lifts and motorized storage lifts. Corner fans, or a wall fan, are the layout the lift owner on Garage Journal described.
  • There is no fan-rated box, and you will not add one. The Haiku K3150-A2 guide says to use an outlet box suitable for fan support and that a luminaire box is usually not acceptable. The Trak Edge is 66 pounds before it spins. The DualMount and the iLiving exist so you are not hanging that weight from a light box. The DualMount was out of stock.
  • You need the garage cooler, not breezier. Hunter’s garage page says a fan does not lower the temperature. A wet-rated Haiku in a closed, uninsulated Arizona garage in July moves hot air. Insulation, shade, and air conditioning are the temperature project. This site has not priced those.

FAQ

How high do the blades have to be?

ENERGY STAR says at least 7 feet above the floor and 18 inches from the walls, and 8–9 feet above the floor when the ceiling allows. Big Ass Fans’ Haiku K3150-A2 install guide says 7 feet from the floor to the fan and to the airfoils, and 3 feet from obstructions, including doors. That guide is not the Haiku L manual. The Haiku L install PDF was not opened. A 2010 Garage Journal reply remembers a 7-foot line in a Home Depot industrial-fan manual. Use the manual in the box you buy, and use 7 feet as the floor you do not go under.

Will a ceiling fan fit an 8-foot garage?

Only if the lowest blade stays at least 7 feet off the floor after the mount, and the sweep misses the open door. Ferguson lists the Haiku L Outdoor at 11.5 inches high with the 5-inch downrod. On a 96-inch ceiling that is 84.5 inches to the bottom, half an inch over 7 feet, if that dimension is the bottom. The same page also says 11.5 feet. Hunter says an 8-foot ceiling wants a low-profile fan, and the Windbound’s published 12.06-inch low hang calculates to under 7 feet. ENERGY STAR’s better airflow height, 8–9 feet, does not fit in an 8-foot room.

Where do I hang it so it misses the door, the opener, and the racks?

Open the door. Keep the entire blade circle out of the panel, the horizontal track, the opener rail, and the rack. A 52-inch fan needs a 26-inch radius before the extra gap in the manual. A wall-mount opener removes the rail and leaves the door, which is the limit on the opener page. The rack still needs its own clearance, on the overhead-rack page.

Downrod or flush mount?

ENERGY STAR: a downrod puts the blades in the 8–9 foot band on a tall ceiling. A flush or hugger mount is for a ceiling under 8 feet, and it moves less air. The Haiku L Outdoor ships with a 5-inch downrod that both the manufacturer and Ferguson assign to 8–9 foot ceilings. The Trak Edge ships with 11 inches, which Hunter’s own 8-foot rule argues against. This check did not find a verified flush-mount garage fan with a CFM and a price worth putting in the table.

Damp-rated or wet-rated in a hot climate?

Hunter’s garage collection page: damp if the garage is humid and covered, wet if the fan can get rained on. The Haiku L Outdoor is the wet-rated ceiling fan verified here. The Trak Edge and the DualMount are damp-rated. The Minka H2O page did not say. The iLiving listing says UL/cUL and indoor/outdoor, which is not the same label as a ceiling-fan wet rating. Opening the door in a storm is the test. If rain can hit the motor, wet. If the fan sits under a solid roof and only sees humidity and dust, damp is the rating Hunter describes.

What span for a 2-car garage versus a 3-car?

ENERGY STAR’s chart puts 50–54 inch fans in rooms of 225–400 square feet. A 20-by-20-foot 2-car bay is 400 square feet. Hunter rates the 72-inch Trak Edge for over 400 square feet. The 84-inch Minka is the wider fan, at 7,880 CFM against the Trak Edge’s 16,662.75 CFM. One 52-inch Haiku is the wet-rated pick for a bay that is about one room of that chart. A 3-car garage wants the larger fan, a taller ceiling, and a place to put the sweep, or it wants more than one fan. A second fan was not priced.

Ceiling fan, wall fan, or plug-in?

A ceiling fan circulates the bay if the blades fit. A wall fan points at you and stays out of the door. The plug-in options verified here are the Hunter DualMount, a 10-foot cord, out of stock at $249.99, and the iLiving wall fan, in stock at $131.85. The Haiku, the Trak Edge, and the Minka are hardwired. Do not add a cord to them.
